01 — Relevance

Running a small business, in practice

Written for AI training and evaluation work on small business operations — the decisions below are ones I've actually had to make and live with.

  • I've run venues, not just worked in them. General manager of a whole izakaya operation for close to two years. Branch manager of a third site while still working the main one. And a four-month interim contract where I took a bar over outright, ran it end to end, then closed the contract out by handing the business on as a going concern with a goodwill premium paid at transfer.
  • Pricing and margin, with the real numbers. Sashimi is priced off an invoice, but more than half the fish is waste once it's broken down. I structured eight months of receipts into 163 items, normalized them so different pack sizes could be compared, and applied a per-species yield of 40–60% so the cost figure reflects what you can actually sell. Menu prices then get set against that, not against the invoice.
  • Vendor management. Five suppliers, 163 line items, eight months of history. I can tell when a supplier's price has drifted before anyone mentions it, and I know how much leverage a small operator actually has when it does.
  • Staffing and compliance without an HR department. Statutory leave accrual under the Labor Standards Act, hourly-rate and ordinary-wage math for part-time staff, and health-certificate expiry tracking because missing one is a fine. Nobody was going to do that for us, so I built the tools that do it.
  • Deciding under a resource constraint is the entire job. No IT budget, no developer, and 18 staff who were never required to adopt anything. I shipped the tools anyway, and three people chose to keep using them daily. Where that landed and why is a more useful thing to reason about than a headline adoption number.
  • I can explain the reasoning, not only the outcome. I write my own work up as problem, decision, result, and what I'd still question — see the "what I saw" note on the ordering app below, where adoption split by age and I report the number that survived a free choice rather than the flattering one. That habit is most of what this kind of evaluation work asks for.
  • Native Korean, and a domain models get wrong quietly. On fish handling, yields, drink specs or how a Korean restaurant is actually staffed and paid, I can say where an answer is wrong and why — not just that it reads oddly.

02 — Works

Four apps that started as problems on my own shift

All of them are a single HTML file backed by browser storage, so a restaurant can adopt one without a server. Every demo has had supplier names, prices and staff names replaced with invented values. The demo interfaces are in Korean; the write-ups here explain what each screen does.

Balju Faster — ordering by voice

In use

6 restaurants · 3 supplier formats · live since July 2026

Problem
Placing an order means walking the walk-in and the dry store and counting what's on the shelf. With around 140 items, you can't look and write at the same time. Hands in gloves or wet, nobody wants to pull out a phone and type, so people did a lap and then wrote the order up from memory. Suppliers wanted different formats on top of that — one takes a sentence over KakaoTalk, another takes a handwritten order sheet — so everything got copied out twice.
Solution
Now you walk, you talk, and it's saved where you're standing. I wrote the parser myself so Korean spoken quantities land as numbers — "one case of bean sprouts", "three hundred grams" — across about 40 unit mappings. On devices where the browser blocks speech recognition it falls back to Gemini audio. Typing stayed in, because sometimes typing is faster and taking it away would have been a worse app. The same order then comes out two ways on its own: a message to paste into KakaoTalk, and an image of the handwritten order sheet.
Result
Stock gets counted and ordered in one pass. The second lap is gone, and so is writing the sheet by hand to photograph it. It's open to 18 staff across six restaurants, and with nobody required to use it, three have used it every shift for two months.
What I saw
Adoption split by age. Younger staff switched immediately; the rest kept their old method. There was never a rule making anyone use it, so the honest number is the one that survived a free choice, and that's the number I report.

Reservation dashboard — parsing the inbox

In use

6 restaurants · Gmail → Apps Script → Sheets → web · live since July 2026

Problem
Reservations arrived as email, scattered across six restaurants. Every morning someone opened the inbox and copied out times, party sizes and names by hand. Just working out when each restaurant's first booking was took real time.
Solution
A Google Apps Script parses the reservation emails into a spreadsheet, and this screen reads the sheet and lays it out per restaurant. Then I added the things people actually ask during service — covers against the same weekday last week and the same nth weekday last month, parties of ten or more flagged, a pending / arrived / no-show status, and a closing-duty rota that rotates itself.
Result
The morning transcription is gone. First booking and large parties for all six restaurants sit on one screen.

Price tracker — what suppliers actually charge

In use

163 items · 5 suppliers · 8 months of order history

Problem
Supplier prices only existed on paper receipts, so nobody could say what had gone up. Sashimi is worse than that: the invoice price isn't the cost. More than half of a fish goes in the bin once it's broken down, and none of that loss was in anyone's cost figure.
Solution
I structured eight months of receipts into 163 items and added month-by-month history, low and high, and direction of travel. Everything normalizes to per-gram, per-ml or per-unit so items sold in different pack sizes can be compared at all. For sashimi it applies a per-species yield of 40–60% to get the true cost of the edible portion, down to the cost of a single slice. Search takes voice too.
Result
"Is this dearer than last month?" is now a five-second question, and sashimi is priced against real cost rather than the invoice.

Multilingual kitchen recipes — KO / PT / VI

In use

54 dishes · 6 groups · 3 languages

Problem
We had cooks who don't read Korean, and the recipes were Korean only. The instructions were also full of kitchen-only shorthand — "long spoon", "t", "knife-rice" — that a translation app turns into nonsense.
Solution
Every step of 54 dishes is written in Portuguese and Vietnamese with the Korean original kept directly underneath, because the talking on the floor still happens in Korean and people need to connect the two. 44 kitchen terms are tappable and give a definition in the reader's own language, with a collapsible glossary alongside. Each card carries a photo of the finished dish that opens full size.
Result
Cooks read the steps in their own language. It's in use now. No native speaker has reviewed the translations yet, and I'd want that before calling it finished.
